What the valve does and why a drip matters

Water heater pressure relief valve replacement in Millburn, NJ homes starts with understanding the part. The temperature and pressure relief valve, or T&P valve, sits on the side or top of the tank and opens if the water gets too hot or the pressure climbs too high. It is the last line of protection when the thermostat or the household pressure fails to hold.

A valve that drips is either worn out or doing its job. Both possibilities need attention, because swapping the valve without finding the cause just gives you a new valve that drips.

Smell gas or see steam, step away

If you smell gas near the heater, leave the building at once, move at least 350 feet away, and then call PSE&G at 1-800-880-7734 or 911. If the valve is blasting steam or scalding water, keep people clear, shut off the heater's power or gas control if you can do so safely, and call the 24/7 line.

Why replacing the valve is not always enough

Household pressure can rise when water heats and expands inside a closed system, where a backflow preventer or pressure reducing valve keeps it from backing into the main. An expansion tank absorbs that, and without one the relief valve may open again and again until it never reseats.

A thermostat that has failed in the on position can also drive the water too hot. In that case the valve is doing exactly what it was built to do, and the fix is the thermostat or the heater.

A licensed plumber doing water heater repair will look at the valve, the discharge pipe, the household pressure and the thermostat in one visit. If the tank is old and rusting, the same conversation often turns to water heater installation of a new unit, since a valve change on a failing tank only delays the replacement.

Signs the valve has failed

What to look for

  • Constant drip or a damp spot under the discharge pipe
  • Mineral crust or green corrosion around the valve body
  • A lever that is stuck, bent or does not snap back
  • No water flows when you lift the lever, since a valve that cannot open cannot protect the tank
  • Water pooling around the base of the heater from a loose or rusted fitting

Safe handling, step by step

  1. Turn off the heat source

    Switch off the breaker for an electric heater, or set a gas heater's control to the off or pilot position.

  2. Close the cold supply and let it cool

    Shut the cold inlet valve and wait for the tank to cool before you touch anything.

  3. Relieve the pressure

    Open a hot tap upstairs, then drain a few gallons from the tank's drain valve so the water level falls below the T&P opening.

  4. Remove the old valve

    Unscrew it with a pipe wrench, take off the discharge pipe, and check the threads for damage.

  5. Install a matching valve

    Choose a valve with the same rating as the one listed on the heater label, wrap the threads with tape or sealant, and tighten snugly.

  6. Refill and test

    Open the cold supply and a hot tap to bleed air, restore power or gas, then check for leaks at the valve and discharge pipe.

Matching the replacement valve to the tank

Valves are not interchangeable. Each one has a pressure setting, a temperature setting, a heat input rating, a thread size and a probe length, and the probe has to reach into the tank water to sense the temperature. Take a photo of the heater's rating plate and the old valve before you shop.

A valve with the wrong rating can open too late or too early. The discharge pipe should also match the valve outlet size, and a plumber will check that it ends where hot water can discharge safely, with no valve, cap or threaded end.

Never cap it

Do not plug, cap or restrict the valve

Never thread a cap onto the valve, seal the discharge pipe, or install a valve in the pipe. A blocked relief valve cannot open, and a tank holding pressure it cannot release is dangerous.

The discharge pipe should run downward without kinks and end where escaping hot water cannot scald anyone.

What you can do and where to stop

A homeowner can reasonably test the lever once or twice a year, done gently while standing clear of the discharge. If water runs freely and the lever snaps back, that is a good sign. If it leaks afterward, the seat may have been scratched by scale.

The valve swap itself is within reach for a handy person with the right wrench, but hot water, gas connections and wet electrical areas raise the stakes. Do not try to cap or repair a valve in place, and call if the heater leaks anywhere else. Neighbors in Summit and Springfield get the same advice. When in doubt, shut the heater off and stop using it.

What changes the price of a valve job

  • Whether the valve alone is bad, or the cause is pressure or a thermostat
  • Whether an expansion tank has to be added or replaced
  • The condition and length of the discharge pipe
  • Corroded fittings or a stuck valve that complicates removal
  • Gas or electric controls that need to be checked
  • Whether the tank is old enough that replacing the heater makes more sense

Millburn, NJ: the plumbing code and permits

New Jersey's plumbing subcode is based on the National Standard Plumbing Code, which the state's Department of Community Affairs adopts. That is the framework behind how relief valves and their discharge pipes are installed.

Permit rules vary, so ask the Millburn building department whether a valve replacement, a discharge pipe change or a full heater swap needs a permit. Do not assume that a small job is exempt.

The takeaway is to ask before you start, and to keep the heater's rating label handy. The label tells the clerk and the plumber exactly what valve rating the tank needs.

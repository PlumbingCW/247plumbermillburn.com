How to Plumb a Tub in Millburn, NJ Homes

A bathtub is two systems sharing one wall. This guide follows the water in and out, and marks the jobs that stay with a licensed plumber.

How a tub is plumbed, in one picture

To plumb a tub in Millburn, NJ, you connect two separate systems. Fresh water arrives through hot and cold supply lines to a valve in the wall and leaves through the spout or showerhead. Used water leaves through a drain and overflow, drops through a trap and runs into a vented drain line.

Everything visible is trim. The real work sits behind the wall and under the floor. That is why swapping a spout or stopper is a homeowner task, while moving a tub or changing its drain is not.

The drain side

Drain and overflow

At the low end of the tub sits the drain shoe, with a strainer or stopper on top. A second opening higher on the end wall is the overflow, which catches water before it spills over the rim. Both connect below the tub through a short tee.

The trap

From the tee, the pipe drops into a P-trap, a curved section that always holds a little water. That water seal blocks sewer gas from rising through the tub. A dry or leaking trap is a common source of a musty bathroom smell.

The vent

Past the trap, the drain line connects to a vent that runs up through the roof. The vent lets air in behind the water so the drain flows freely and the trap seal does not get pulled out. A gurgling tub and a slow drain together often point at a vent or main drain problem.

The supply side

Hot and cold lines feed a mixing valve behind the wall. Modern valves are usually single-handle pressure-balancing or thermostatic units, which help keep the temperature steady when a toilet flushes or a tap opens elsewhere. Inside is a replaceable cartridge, and that cartridge is where most drips and temperature complaints start.

From the valve, water goes up to the showerhead or out to the tub spout. A diverter, either in the spout or on the valve, decides which. When the diverter wears out, water dribbles from the shower while the tub is running. A worn cartridge or trim is a classic job for faucet repair.

Signs the plumbing behind the tub needs attention

Symptoms worth acting on

  • A stain or damp patch on the ceiling or wall below the tub, which often means a leaking overflow gasket or drain connection.
  • A musty smell from the tub when it has not been used, which can point to a dry trap.
  • Water that pools around the drain long after the tub is emptied, a sign of a partial clog or poor slope.
  • Hot water that suddenly turns scalding or cold when a toilet flushes, usually a worn or stuck valve cartridge.
  • Soft or discolored tile and grout near the valve, where a slow leak may be soaking the wall.

None of these needs panic, but none should be ignored for long. A slow leak behind a tiled wall can rot framing before any puddle appears, so have it traced early.

Roughing in a tub, in order

  1. Set the tub and drain

    The tub is placed on a firm, level base, and the drain and overflow are connected beneath it with watertight joints.

  2. Connect the trap and drain line

    The trap ties into the branch drain at the correct slope, and that branch connects to the vent.

  3. Run the supply lines

    Hot and cold lines go to the valve body, which is fixed at the right depth for the finished wall.

  4. Pressure test

    Lines are checked for leaks before anything is closed up behind tile or an access panel.

  5. Install the trim

    Spout, handle and stopper go on last, once the wall surface is finished.

An access panel saves a wall

When a tub backs onto a closet or hallway, an access panel behind the valve makes later repairs far easier. Ask a plumber about adding one before the wall is finished.

Millburn, NJ: the plumbing subcode and the permit question

New Jersey's plumbing subcode is based on the National Standard Plumbing Code, which the state's Department of Community Affairs adopts. A new edition was reported adopted in 2024. That is the rulebook a licensed plumber works to when drains, vents and valves are changed.

What it means for your tub is simple. Permit rules vary, so ask the Millburn building department before moving a drain, adding a bathroom or touching the vent. Cosmetic swaps like a spout or stopper usually raise no question, but a quick call costs nothing. Do not assume that a DIY drain change meets code just because it holds water.

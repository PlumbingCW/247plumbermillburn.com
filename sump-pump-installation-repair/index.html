Sump pump or sewage ejector, which one is yours

The two look alike from the stairs, but they do different jobs. A sump pump removes groundwater from a pit, usually with a drain tile connection. A sewage ejector lifts wastewater and solids from a basement bathroom, laundry or sink up to the sewer line. Treating one like the other is how people end up with the wrong repair.

Sump pump and sewage ejector compared
FeatureSump pumpSewage ejector
What it handlesGroundwater and storm seepageWastewater and solids from basement fixtures
BasinOpen or lidded pit, often tied to drain tileSealed basin with a gasketed lid and a vent
DischargeOutside, away from the foundationInto the home's drain line
SmellNone in normal useOdor means a lid, seal or vent problem
Failure signWater at the floor edge, constant runningSlow drains or sewage backing up in the basement

Sewage ejector replacement follows the same logic as a sump swap. The pump comes out of the sealed basin, a new one goes in with a new check valve if needed, and the lid and vent are checked. A cracked basin or a bad lid seal is a bigger repair.

Choosing a pump, check valve and discharge

A submersible pump sits in the pit, runs quietly and handles being under water. A pedestal pump keeps its motor above the pit, which makes it easier to service but louder and more exposed. Either can work, and the pit size, the amount of water and your budget decide which one fits.

The check valve sits on the discharge pipe and stops water in the line from dropping back into the pit. When it fails, the pump short cycles and you hear a thump as water falls back. Discharge routing matters too. The water must go well away from the foundation or it will return.

Rules on where the discharge may go vary, so ask the Millburn building department before changing the route. Do not point it at a neighbor or toward the foundation of your own house.

What a sound sump set-up has

  • Pit large enough for the float to move freely
  • Check valve on the discharge line
  • Discharge routed away from the house
  • Outlet on its own protected circuit
  • Lid on the pit to cut humidity and odor

Battery backup for storm power cuts

Storms that bring heavy rain also tend to knock out power, and a pump with no power is just a bucket. A battery backup runs a second pump, or the main pump, when the outlet goes dead.

Its battery has limited capacity and needs periodic testing and replacement, so it buys time rather than solving a flood. Make that decision before the next storm, not during it. A plumber can size the backup to your pit and tell you how long it should run.

Testing the pump and reading the float switch

  1. Check the power

    Confirm the pump is plugged into a working outlet and that any GFCI has not tripped.

  2. Fill the pit

    Pour a bucket of water slowly into the pit until the float rises.

  3. Watch the cycle

    The pump should start, empty the pit and shut off without chattering.

  4. Listen at shutoff

    A hard thump as the pit refills means the check valve is not holding.

  5. Look at the float

    It should rise and fall freely, not rest against the pit wall or the pump.

Unplug the pump before reaching into the pit. If it fails any of these checks, call. A pump that runs but moves little water is as bad as one that does not run.

A pump that runs constantly usually has a float stuck up, a check valve letting water fall back, or too little pump for the water arriving. One that never runs may have lost power, have a float stuck low, or have a burned-out motor. The test above separates these quickly, which is why it belongs on the calendar before each wet season.

Signs the pit needs service

Do not wait for the next storm

  • Water in the pit that stays high after a storm has passed
  • A pump that turns on in dry weather
  • Grinding, rattling or a motor that hums without pumping
  • Sludge, gravel or rust visible in the pit
  • A frayed cord or a cracked discharge pipe
  • Dampness or odor in the basement returning each season

A pump that works but cycles constantly in wet weather may be undersized for the water in your yard. Add basement waterproofing to the conversation when the water keeps arriving faster than the pump can move it, and use leak detection when the pit fills on dry days, since a supply leak can look like groundwater.

Bill climbing, meter turning, ceiling damp or floor warm?

Water that is running, soaking a ceiling or spreading across a floor is an emergency, and our 24/7 line takes leak detection calls in Millburn, NJ at any hour. Shut the main water valve first, switch off power to any wet area you can reach safely, and move belongings out of the drip path.

Most people notice a symptom long before they know the word for the service. The water bill climbs with no change in habits, the meter keeps turning with every fixture off, a ceiling stain spreads, a floor feels warm underfoot, or pressure sags at every faucet.

Water can travel a long way from the break before it shows. Leak detection is the work of finding the actual spot, which keeps the opening in the wall or floor small.

When a leak is an emergency

A slow stain that has not changed in weeks can wait for a same-day visit, but it still deserves an answer. Some signs cannot wait.

Call right away when you see

  • Water visibly running or dripping from a ceiling
  • A ceiling that sags or bulges
  • Water near outlets, switches or the electrical panel
  • A meter that keeps moving after the main valve is closed

Stop the water, then call

Close the main shutoff, open one low faucet to drain the lines, and stay out from under a bulging ceiling. Never touch light fixtures, switches or outlets that are wet or dripping.

Night and weekend calls are handled the same way as daytime ones. The plumber's first job is to stop the damage, then locate the source.

How leak detection finds the leak

No single method works on every leak, so a plumber combines them and narrows the search step by step. The goal is to point at one spot on the pipe before anyone opens a wall.

  1. Isolate with a shutoff test

    Closing valves zone by zone shows which part of the system still loses water, such as the hot side, the cold side or the line to one bathroom.

  2. Pressure test the line

    The plumber pressurizes an isolated section and watches the gauge. A drop means a leak somewhere in that run.

  3. Listen with acoustic equipment

    Sensitive microphones pick up the hiss of water escaping under pressure through floors, walls and soil, and the loudest point marks the likely spot.

  4. Scan for heat and moisture

    Thermal imaging shows temperature differences, such as a warm patch over a hot line or a cold wet area behind drywall, and a moisture meter confirms it.

None of these promises a find on every job. Together they make a miss much less likely and let the repair start at the right place.

Doing a meter test at home

A meter test takes a few minutes and tells you whether a leak exists at all. It also tells you which side of the home main it is on.

  1. Stop all water use

    Close every tap, shut off the ice maker and dishwasher, and make sure nobody flushes.

  2. Read the meter

    Find it in the basement or in a box near the curb and note the dial. Many meters have a small low-flow indicator that moves with the tiniest flow.

  3. Wait, then read again

    Leave the water alone for an hour or so. A dial or indicator that has moved means water is escaping.

  4. Close the home main

    Shut the main valve inside the house and watch again. If the meter keeps moving, the leak is between the meter and that valve.

If the meter stops when the main is closed, the leak is inside the house, and a toilet is the first suspect. Drop food coloring into the tank and see whether it shows in the bowl without flushing.

Ceiling and wall leaks

A stain on a ceiling rarely sits under the leak. Water follows framing and pipes before it drips, so the source may be a few feet away or a floor above. Timing helps. A stain that grows after rain points to the roof, one that grows after showers points to a bathroom above, and one that never stops growing points to a pressurized supply line.

Condensation on a cold pipe or a clogged air conditioner drain pan can look the same, which is why the plumber checks before cutting. A moisture meter and a thermal scan map the wet area so the access hole goes where the water is. Switch off power to any ceiling light that is wet.

Basement and crawlspace leaks

Look low and look for the telltale signs. White crust or green staining at pipe joints, rust on fittings, wet insulation, a damp spot below a hot water tank and a floor that stays wet on dry days all point to plumbing. A basement that wets only after storms usually points to groundwater.

That difference matters in Millburn. When the remnants of Ida hit in September 2021, basements in the South Mountain area filled with water and sewage, and floodwater and a pipe leak call for different fixes.

If your sump pit runs constantly with no rain, the cause may be a leaking supply line, and sump pump installation and repair should come with a check of the pipes. Crawlspaces under additions get the same inspection, with extra attention to pipes hanging in cold air.

Buried and service line leaks

The water line from the street runs underground, so a break shows up as a meter that moves with the home main closed, a soft or unusually green patch of ground, a sound of running water, or weak pressure. Listening equipment follows the sound along the pipe to the loudest point.

Ownership decides who fixes it. NJ American Water owns the service line from the main to the curb stop, and the property owner owns the rest from the curb stop into the building.

Slab leaks, and where they apply

A slab leak is a break in a line that runs under or inside a concrete floor. Whether it applies to your home depends on how the house is built, so check what your floors sit on. In a house with a basement it is uncommon in the main living area, but slabs do show up under additions, garages, sunrooms and some lower-level rooms.

Signs are a warm area on the floor, the sound of running water with fixtures off, damp flooring or a meter that turns. The plumber locates the line first. Options range from a spot repair through a small opening to rerouting the pipe overhead so the slab stays intact, and the right one depends on what the survey finds. If a pipe has failed outright, see burst pipe repair.

Sprinklers, pools, meter boxes and the repair itself

Irrigation. A zone that stays soggy, a sprinkler head that never stops dripping or a sudden pressure drop when the system runs points to a buried irrigation leak. Close the irrigation valve and retest the meter. If the meter stops, the leak is in that system and the plumber can pressure test each zone.

Pools and spas. A pool that loses more than evaporation explains may have a leak in its supply or return plumbing. A bucket test separates evaporation from loss, and the plumbing between the equipment and the house can then be pressure tested.

Yard and meter box. Water pooling around the meter box, or a box that fills, can be on either side of the curb stop, and that line decides who repairs it. Tell the dispatcher what you see.

Repair. Once the spot is found, the fix may be a section of pipe, a new fitting or a reroute. If the pipe keeps failing in different places, the conversation turns to repiping rather than another patch.

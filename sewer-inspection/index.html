What a sewer camera inspection shows

A sewer camera inspection in Millburn, NJ sends a small waterproof camera through your sewer line and shows the pipe from the inside. You see the material, the joints, any cracks or gaps, roots, buildup, low spots that hold water and the place where the line meets the street connection.

It answers questions no one can settle from the surface, such as whether the pipe is cast iron, clay or plastic, whether a clog is a one-time event or a sign of damage, and whether lining is even possible. The answers turn a guess into a plan.

What the camera can reveal

  • Pipe material and approximate condition
  • Cracks, breaks and offset joints
  • Roots entering at joints
  • Grease, scale or debris coating the walls
  • Sags or bellies that hold standing water
  • Collapsed or crushed sections

When to book an inspection

The best time is before a problem forces your hand. Buyers of older homes in Millburn, Short Hills and nearby towns such as Summit often want to know the condition of the line before closing, because a sewer repair can be a large cost that is easy to avoid by learning about it first. The same logic applies if you inherit or take over a property.

Book one after repeated clogs in the same line, after a backup has been cleared, or when slow drains show up in several fixtures. A line that was cleaned but never looked at can fail again for the same reason. After a flood or heavy storm, a look confirms nothing shifted.

Ask for the footage

Ask that the findings are shown to you and explained in plain words, with any damaged sections pointed out. That is what lets you compare repair options later.

How the visit works

  1. Find the way in

    The camera enters through a cleanout. If the house has none, the plumber may use a roof vent or pull a toilet.

  2. Clear what is in the way

    A heavily clogged line may need cleaning before the camera can pass, which is why cleaning and inspection are often done together.

  3. Run the camera

    The plumber feeds the cable toward the street, watching the screen for defects and noting where each one sits.

  4. Locate the problem

    A locator at the surface can mark the depth and path of the line above ground, which helps plan any digging.

  5. Talk through the findings

    You see what the camera saw, and the plumber explains what it means and what can wait.

A single finding is rarely a verdict. Ask whether the problem is localized or runs along the line, whether it is getting worse and what happens if you wait.

What a good report includes

Whatever is written down should let a second plumber understand the line without a second visit. Ask for these items.

  1. The address and the date of the inspection
  2. The pipe material and size where it could be seen
  3. The location of each defect measured from the cleanout or a reference point
  4. A plain description of each finding
  5. The recommended next step for each one
  6. Whether any section could not be inspected and why

A report that says only that the line looks fine is not enough. A line that cannot be passed by the camera is a finding in itself.

What happens next

The result usually points to one of four paths. A line with buildup and no damage gets cleaned through sewer line cleaning, sometimes with hydro jetting. A line with a localized defect gets a spot repair or a liner, and a line with long runs of damage may need replacement. Many lines need nothing but a record that they are sound.

When the camera finds damage, the next conversation is sewer line repair, including the options and their limits. Having the findings in hand means you are choosing between methods and not guessing at them.
